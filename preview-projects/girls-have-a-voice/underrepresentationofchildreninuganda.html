A rights-based participation initiative · Uganda

Decisions about girls should include girls.

Girls have insight into the decisions shaping their education, safety, health, and futures. This initiative helps schools, communities, civil society, and public institutions listen to girls and reflect their views in decisions.

✳ Meaningful participation is safe, inclusive, voluntary, and followed by feedback.

01 · The issue

Being affected by a decision is not the same as having a say in it.

Decisions at home, in schools, health services, and local communities shape girls’ education, safety, wellbeing, and opportunities. Yet girls may have limited access to the spaces where these decisions are discussed or made.

Girls are not a single, uniform group. Age, disability, location, language, family circumstances, and other experiences can affect whose views are heard. Meaningful representation makes space for this diversity and shows girls how their contributions were considered.

Participation is meaningful when children can express views freely and receive a clear response about how those views were considered. — A practical interpretation of children’s participation rights

02 · A shared standard

From presence to influence.

Strong participation is a process—not a one-off consultation. These principles can guide planning, facilitation, decision-making, and follow-up.

01

Accessible and inclusive

Offer different ways to contribute and address barriers related to language, disability, age, location, time, and access to information.

02

Safe and voluntary

Explain the purpose, protect privacy, avoid pressure, and make sure children know they can decline to answer or stop taking part.

03

Respectful and honest

Use child-friendly language, listen without judgement, and be clear about which decisions can—and cannot—be changed.

04

Representative

Avoid relying on a small, familiar group. Consider whose views are missing and invite participation through appropriate channels.

05

Influential

Give children’s views genuine consideration and document how those views informed a decision or why a proposal was not adopted.

06

Accountable

Share outcomes with children, invite their reflections, and improve the process based on what they say worked or did not work.

03 · Understanding barriers

Representation takes more than an invitation.

Barriers differ between settings and communities. Ask children and local partners what is relevant rather than assuming that one approach will work for everyone.

  • A Adult-led decision-making Children may be treated as recipients of services rather than contributors with useful knowledge.
  • B Practical access Meeting times, transport, cost, inaccessible venues, or limited internet access can prevent participation.
  • C Unequal opportunities to speak Some children may be overlooked because of age, disability, language, gender, poverty, or social exclusion.
  • D Fear or lack of trust Children may not feel safe disagreeing with adults or may worry that their views will not remain private.
  • E No feedback after consultation When children hear nothing afterward, participation can feel tokenistic and trust can be weakened.

04 · A practical action plan

Build participation into the way decisions are made.

Begin with one decision-making setting—such as a school, youth programme, community group, or service—and adapt the steps with children and local safeguarding partners.

Listen and map

Identify decisions that affect children. Ask who currently contributes, who is missing, and what would make participation safe and accessible.

Co-design the process

Work with children to choose topics, formats, ground rules, and support needs. Offer small-group, written, creative, and private ways to share views.

Share decisions

Explain which choices are open for discussion. Record the views shared and show how they were considered in the final decision.

Report back and improve

Tell children what happened, what changed, and why. Ask whether the process felt fair and use their feedback to improve it.

Start with a manageable activity, review it with participants, and expand only when appropriate safeguarding and follow-up are in place.

05 · Learning and evaluation

Measure the quality of participation, not just attendance.

A sign-in sheet can show who was present, but not whether children felt safe, were listened to, or influenced an outcome. Use a mix of observations and child-friendly feedback, and avoid collecting personal information that is not needed.

  • Access Could children with different needs and backgrounds take part?
  • Confidence and safety Did participants understand their choices and feel able to speak freely?
  • Influence Can decision-makers describe how children’s views were considered?
  • Feedback Did children receive an understandable response about the outcome?
  • Improvement What would children change about the next participation activity?

A non-negotiable foundation

Participation must protect children’s dignity and safety.

Every activity should follow relevant local child-protection procedures and the safeguarding policies of the organisations involved. Participation is not a reason to ask children to disclose personal or distressing experiences.

  • Explain the purpose and how information will be used.
  • Seek appropriate consent and respect a child’s choice not to participate.
  • Protect confidentiality and explain its limits before an activity.
  • Use trained, trusted adults and clear reporting procedures.
  • Do not promise secrecy when a child may be at risk of harm.
  • Provide a safe way to raise concerns or ask for support.
